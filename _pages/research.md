# Patrolling the Powerful: The Reputational Consequences of Punishing Power-Holders
Currently under Revise and Resubmit at the *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*

Powerful leaders sometimes behave unethically, yet their followers may be reluctant to confront their wrongdoings because doing so can expose them to significant risks of retaliation. Nevertheless, people do sometimes stand up to harmful leaders. In an archival study of more than 14,000 federal workers in the United States, we found that when these workers had witnessed their supervisors perpetrating a serious incident of workplace aggression or harassment, they confronted them nearly 25% of the time. Strikingly, they were not the victims of these misconduct incidents. Rather, they were bystanders who chose to confront their bosses on behalf of others. In joint work with my postdoctoral advisor, [Ena Inesi](https://www.london.edu/faculty-and-research/faculty-profiles/i/inesi-me/), we examine why people take such risks. We hypothesise that one important reason is driven by reputation: people who confront unethical leaders in this way earn strong moral reputations, which yield other downstream rewards. We investigate this hypothesis across a series of experiments.

# Right to Brag? The Reputational Consequences of Disclosing Prosocial Behavior
Currently under review at the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*

Research has shown that people's reputations suffer when they tell others about their good deeds. Rather than seeing these individuals as moral exemplars, we see them as self-promoters who are trying to reap benefits from advertising their prosocial acts. However, what we know in this area is based on people bragging about unambiguously good deeds, like donating to charity. But good deeds come in more ambiguous forms. Imagine that you see a stranger attempting to steal someone else’s bicycle, then you decide to confront them. This is an example of a third-party punishment. This action may be confrontational, but it is also prosocial: by intervening, you protect someone else's property and uphold social norms against theft. Research has shown that punishers are seen as dominant and aggressive; but also trustworthy, cooperative, and moral. In other words, punishment signals a mixed profile of traits. In joint work with [Jonathan Berman](https://www.london.edu/faculty-and-research/faculty-profiles/b/berman-j-z-1) and my postdoctoral advisor, [Ena Inesi](https://www.london.edu/faculty-and-research/faculty-profiles/i/inesi-me), we suggest that when people brag about their punishments, their reputations often benefit rather than suffer. We don't see these individuals as self-promoters; instead we infer that they are simply trying to warn us about the threatening incidents that prompted their interventions (e.g., there may be a bicycle thief on the loose!). In a series of experiments, we explore the motive-attributions and moral character evaluations that participants make when they hear others bragging about their punishments to colleagues and on social media.

# More Than a Consensus? A Social Relations Model Analysis of Status
Currently under review at the *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*

In social psychology, status refers to the respect and admiration a person receives relative to others in a social group. Status plays a pivotal role in social interactions: for instance, it shapes people's opportunities to participate in decision-making and exert influence over others. For decades, researchers have asked a fundamental question: *Why do some people attain higher status than others?* The prevailing answer centres on two factors: groups confer status on those they perceive as (a) competent and (b) committed to advancing the group's interests. However, judgments of competence and prosocial motivation are not always shared. The same individual may be judged to be highly capable by one person but far less capable by others. This is because status judgments are formed through interpersonal relationships. Despite this, scholars have devoted relatively little attention to understanding how relationships shape status judgments. In this project—the central component of my dissertation, developed under the mentorship of my PhD advisors, [Sebastien Brion](https://www.iese.edu/faculty-research/faculty/sebastien-brion/) and [Anneloes Raes](https://www.iese.edu/faculty-research/faculty/anneloes-raes/)—we examine just how much relationships matter for status judgments. We use a statistical approach that separates interpersonal perceptions into their underlying sources and identify the circumstances in which relationship effects are strongest. Our findings not only quantify the importance of relationship effects in status evaluations, but they also show that these effects are weakest among status rivals—that is, people who occupy similar space in the hierarchy.
